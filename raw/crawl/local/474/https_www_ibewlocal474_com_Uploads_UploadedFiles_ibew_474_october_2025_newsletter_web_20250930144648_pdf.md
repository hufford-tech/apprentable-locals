# RETIREMENT - IN-SERVICE DISTRIBUTION

On January 1, 2026, the NECA-IBEW Memphis Retirement Plan (The “Plan”) will be amended as follows: 
Under Article III, Eligibility for Benefits, a new section 3.4 titled “In-Service Distribution” shall be added.
Section 3.4 In-Service Distribution

Effective January 1, 2026, an Employee, who has attained the age of 60 and on whose behalf the Trustees maintain an 
Individual Account, may request an “In-Service Distribution” benefit from the Employee’s Individual Account. The 
“In-Service Distribution” benefit may only be requested at the beginning of each calendar year and is limited to $25,000 
or less. An Employee is not required to cease employment to be eligible for the “In-Service Distribution” benefit.
Under Article 4, Section 4.2 Method of Payment, a new subsection (g) shall be added as follows:

## (g) In-Service Distribution

*Notwithstanding any other provision herein, an Employee who has attained the age of 60, but has not yet ceased* 
*employment, shall have the option to request a distribution of $25,000 or less from the Employee’s Accumulated Share at the* 
*beginning of each calendar year. In the event the Employee has a Spouse, the informed consent of the Employee’s Spouse will be* 
*required in order for the Employee to elect to receive an In-Service Distribution of the Employee’s Accumulated Share. Such* 
*consent must be in writing and must be witnessed by a Notary Public or representative of the Plan.*

## OUR HISTORY

The history of the IBEW in Memphis, Tennessee stretches back to 
January 6, 1892 when the National Brotherhood of Electrical Workers 
First Grand President Henry Miller, who was the founding father of the 
brotherhood, traveled to Memphis from St. Louis and organized NBEW, 
Local 6 to fight for safer working conditions and better wages for 
Electrical workers in Memphis. In those early years the unionized 
electrical industry faced stiff opposition, particularly in the southern 
states, and Local Union 6 later went defunct due to a lack of membership. 
Over the years prior to Local 474 there were three additional Locals that 
were chartered and later went defunct, those being Local 14, Local 50 and 
Local 192. When the brotherhood expanded into Canada the NBEW 
changed it's name to the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers 
in 1899. IBEW Local 474 received it's  charter on January 13, 1906 and has 
since grown to be a diverse Local union representing workers in varying 
industries other than construction such as electrical manufacturing, 
government services and electrical cooperatives.

# MESSAGE FROM THE BUSINESS MANAGER

To all our Union Brothers and Sisters. Everyone has heard the saying, “Elections have consequences”, and that has never been more 
evident than in today’s current environment. For many years, Labor has been attacked by politicians seeking to diminish any power 
held by Unions. From “Right to Work” laws to stripping federal workers of Union representation, the anti-union crusaders have tried 
everything they can think of. Now Memphis is facing the same type of assault from a supposed labor-friendly Democratic Mayor, 
Paul Young.

In 1963, Memphis Sanitation Workers began fighting for Union representation, but the City refused to recognize them. After five 
long years, on February 12, 1968, the Sanitation Workers went on strike. On March 18th Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. came to Memphis 
to speak to the Sanitation Workers and show his support for their efforts. He came again on March 28th. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. 
made a third trip to show his support on April 3, 1968, when he made his now famous, “I’ve been to the mountaintop” speech. He 
was assassinated the next day. After that, President Lyndon B. Johnson dispatched James Reynolds, Undersecretary of Labor, to 
Memphis to help mediate and negotiate a resolution. On April 16, 1968, the Memphis City Council ultimately recognized the 
American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees (AFSCME) Local 1733 as the Sanitation Workers' union 
representation. That first, ever important labor agreement, known as an MOU (Memorandum of Understanding), has laid the 
groundwork for other city employees to have Union representation to this day.

1968 – AFSCME was recognized and recognition procedures were established.

1969 – Memphis City Council gave employees the right to form and join Unions. Machinist Union was recognized. Memphis Light, 
Gas and Water Division IBEW Local 1288 was recognized.

1971 – International Association of Fire Fighters Local 1784 was recognized. Operating Engineers were recognized.

1972 – Various craft Unions were recognized (there are now six in all). An additional Operating Engineers' Local was recognized.
1973 – Memphis Police Association was recognized.

There are now a total of thirteen Unions recognized by the City of Memphis.

In 1978, the City of Memphis adopted an “Impasse Ordinance” as a means of resolving contract negotiation impasses between the 
city and its municipal workers. The “Impasse Ordinance” was put on the ballot and the citizens of Memphis exercised their right to 
vote for and pass that ordinance. Since 1968, every Mayor of Memphis, from then until now has honored the MOU’s and then the 
“Impasse Ordinance” that was voted on by the citizens.

Now Mayor Paul Young’s office has filed a lawsuit with the City to invalidate and do away with all public unions’ (MOU’s) 
Memorandum of Understanding, in the City of Memphis. As Union members, we cannot sit idly by and let this happen. We must 
get involved, we must fight for the right to representation, because our Union’s very existence depends on it. IBEW Local 474 does 
have members working under these same MOU’s that Mayor Paul Young is trying to invalidate, and if he is able to do away with one, 
he will be able to do away with them all. If that happens, it will set a precedence for other cities in Tennessee to do the same. For any 
non-members working under these MOU’s, it is time to join the Union and join the fight, because they will also be affected if this 
happens.

The fight for civil rights, human rights, and the right to Union Representation is what Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. fought for and 
ultimately died for. Many others have fought and died for those same rights. That fight, for those rights, is what built the very 
platform that Paul Young stood upon when he campaigned for Mayor as a “labor-friendly” candidate. We cannot let him tear down 
the very legacy that allowed him to rise to the position he is in today. We must let him know that we, as Union members, will not 
stand for it. When the time comes, we must accept the challenge to stand in Solidarity with all of our Union Brothers and Sisters in 
the face of opposition, for the future of our Union. Because the future of our Union depends on it.

Yours in Brotherhood,

# Noel Sherman
